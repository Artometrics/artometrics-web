INSTITUTIONS: When Dynasties Are Systems

Three Artometrics reports already established the raw numbers: the Yankees' 27 World Series titles, the Patriots' six Super Bowls in 19 seasons, and the Celtics' 18 NBA championships, the league record entering 2025. None of those reports asked the question this one does — what, if anything, transfers across sports when a dynasty outlives the star who built it, and where does spending alone fail to substitute for it.

This is a meta report. It introduces no new dataset. It rereads three already-published Artometrics analyses — the cross-league sports-dynasty index, the Patriots' system-shock report, and the Celtics' institutional-winning report — against each other, plus, for supporting context only, four sibling franchise reports already in the Artometrics archive (Lakers, Warriors, Dodgers, Padres). The result is a simple typology: star-gravity dynasties, system dynasties, and spending-without-conversion franchises.

The test case sits inside the Patriots' own numbers. Their six championships arrived across 19 seasons and nine Super Bowl appearances — a 55 percent conversion rate, the highest among the dynasty-tier franchises the index compares. Then the system's namesake star left. The Celtics offer the opposite kind of evidence: an 18-title archive that survived a 22-year gap between 1986 and 2008 and still produced a 2024 championship, under a fifth distinct core generations removed from the era that built the brand.

RESEARCH QUESTION

When championship-winning is treated as an institutional system rather than a single roster, what actually carries over from one era to the next — coaching and front-office continuity, institutional culture, or something else — and at what point does financial spending fail to substitute for it?

STAR GRAVITY VS. INSTITUTIONAL CONTINUITY

This is the framework's actual contribution — an editorial typology, not a new measurement. Two of the three source reports describe dynasties whose championship archives outlived their most famous single star. The sports-dynasty index's own sibling pieces on the Lakers ("how the Lakers convert star gravity into title eras") and the Warriors ("the franchise that rewrote basketball offense") describe a different mechanism: championship output that tracks the presence of a specific, dominant talent core. Call that star-gravity. The Patriots and Celtics reports describe something else: system dynasties, where coaching, front-office structure, or multi-generational institutional identity keeps producing championships across rosters that do not share a single defining star. The Patriots won six titles under one quarterback-coach pairing; the Celtics won 18 across five distinct eras — Russell, Havlicek, Bird, Pierce/Garnett, and Tatum/Brown, per the Celtics report's own era buckets — a continuity no single star's career could span.

WHEN SPENDING DOESN'T CONVERT: COWBOYS AND PADRES

The sports-dynasty index's third finding is the cleanest evidence against treating market value as a proxy for competitive output: the Cowboys have won zero Super Bowls since their 1990s dynasty ended in 1996, despite ranking in the top five of NFL franchise valuations every year since. The same report cites the Maple Leafs' 57-season active Stanley Cup drought entering 2025 as the longest among high-value franchises in any of the four leagues it compares. Elsewhere in the Artometrics archive, the Padres report frames the same failure in dollar terms — a reported $3.9 billion in spending against zero championships. None of these three cases lack resources. What they lack, by the sports-dynasty index's own framing, is whatever the Patriots and Celtics reports describe as the system: a repeatable institutional mechanism for converting access — Finals appearances, playoff berths, big-name signings — into titles. Spending buys attention and access. It does not, on this evidence, buy conversion.

THE PATRIOTS TEST: WHAT SURVIVES THE STAR'S DEPARTURE

The Patriots report's own numbers are the strongest system-dynasty case in the corpus: six championships and nine Super Bowl appearances between 2001 and 2019, a 55 percent conversion rate — 6 of 11 all-time Super Bowl appearances — that the sports-dynasty index singles out as the highest among dynasty-tier franchises since 1950. That conversion rate exceeds even the Yankees' 40 percent, despite the Yankees holding more than four times the total championship count. The Patriots report itself frames 2020 — the first season without Tom Brady — as the hinge point where brand capital stayed high while football output did not automatically follow. That report leaves the question open rather than answering it: whether the infrastructure (coaching continuity, personnel evaluation, a specific institutional culture) survives the departure of the star who anchored it. This synthesis does not resolve that question with new data; it names it as the central test case for the star-gravity vs. system distinction, because the Patriots dynasty is the one cited case in the corpus where the star left and the institution kept operating without him.

CELTICS: INSTITUTIONAL IDENTITY ACROSS FIVE ERAS AND A 22-YEAR INTERRUPTION

The Celtics report is the corpus's clearest system-dynasty case precisely because it spans the most time. Eleven of the franchise's 18 championships arrived in the Bill Russell era; the remaining seven were split across four later, unrelated cores — Havlicek, Bird, Pierce/Garnett, and Tatum/Brown — with a 22-year gap between the 1986 and 2008 titles proving that even this franchise can go without a title for over two decades. The most recent championship came in 2024, under a core with no direct roster or personnel link to Russell's team seven decades earlier. What transferred across that span was not a roster, a coach, or even a front-office regime — it was, per the Celtics report's own framing, an institutional standard: every era judged against Russell's conversion rate rather than against league-average competition. That is a different mechanism from the Patriots' case, where continuity ran through one coach and one drafted quarterback. The Celtics case suggests institutional culture alone — a standard the organization is judged against — can be the thing that transfers, independent of any single administration.

LIMITATIONS

This synthesis carries every limitation of its three source reports, plus its own. The sample is small — a handful of North American franchises across four leagues, not a representative draw of professional sports organizations. Survivorship bias is structural: these franchises are covered by Artometrics, and by sports media generally, because they won; franchises that spent heavily and never built any championship-era narrative at all are underrepresented in the corpus this report draws from.

Conversion-rate denominators differ by sport and are not directly comparable on a single scale — a 162-game MLB season followed by a best-of-seven World Series is a different selection mechanism than the NFL's single-elimination Super Bowl path, a difference the sports-dynasty index itself names when describing league rotation patterns. The "years since 1996" figure used for the Cowboys in the drought chart above is a derived count (2026 minus 1996), not a number stated verbatim in the source report, which states only the "zero championships since 1996" fact; treat it as an editorial calculation, not an observed statistic. Finally, star-gravity, system dynasty, and spending-without-conversion are editorial categories proposed here for the first time — they are an interpretive framework, not a measured or tested classification.

CONCLUSION

Across the three source reports, championship mass, conversion efficiency, and institutional durability turn out to be separate variables, not one. The Yankees carry the largest archive without the highest conversion rate; the Patriots carry the highest conversion rate on a much smaller archive; the Celtics carry both a large archive and a proven ability to survive a 22-year interruption. The Cowboys and, per the Padres report, the Padres show that none of this is guaranteed by spending alone.

What the Patriots and Celtics reports jointly suggest — without this synthesis adding any new measurement of its own — is that the thing worth calling a "system" is whatever keeps producing championships after the specific roster, or the specific star, that built the brand is gone. The Patriots case is still an open test, by that report's own account. The Celtics case, spanning five eras and one multi-decade drought, is the corpus's best evidence that the answer can be yes.
